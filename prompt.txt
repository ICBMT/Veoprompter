TRANSITION CLIP SYSTEM
Not every story state can be connected through direct frame-to-frame continuation. Before planning each clip-to-clip handoff, evaluate whether the next state can realistically and reliably continue from the preceding final frame. Classify each incoming handoff as one of these three types and repeat that classification in the plan, the prior clip's outgoing handoff, and the destination clip's `TRANSITION TYPE` metadata line.

TYPE A — DIRECT CONTINUATION
Use only when the next clip naturally continues from the preceding final frame with no hidden state jump: a character continues walking or acting, a hand completes a movement, an object keeps moving, a camera keeps tracking, or a subject moves naturally to a nearby position. The next clip's opening state must match the preceding ending exactly—including every relevant object's identity, location, count, condition, motion, and process stage. Do not restart a completed action, skip an operation, or present a later state as direct continuation. Include the mandatory continuation instruction verbatim.

TYPE B — NATURAL ACTION TRANSITION
Use when a visible, physically plausible action connects the preceding and next states: a character walks from a field into a house, opens and passes through a door, carries an object between nearby locations, or a camera follows the subject from a wide view into a close-up. Show the route, transfer, contact, or operation that actually changes the state; preserve the identity and quantity of transported materials and parts, and never teleport or duplicate a character, object, or workpiece. A motivated cut may change framing, but it must not hide an impossible process step or chronology. Whenever possible, begin from the previous final frame. Include the mandatory continuation instruction when the transition begins directly from that frame; if it does not, do not falsely claim an uninterrupted start.

TYPE C — NARRATIVE / TEMPORAL TRANSITION
Use for a major time, location, or state change that cannot honestly be presented as direct continuation: years passing, seasons changing, a child aging, a seed becoming a mature tree, a building being constructed, a material becoming a finished product, or a distant-location move. Do not describe a Type C transition as an ordinary seamless continuation and do not include the mandatory continuation instruction in its dedicated transition clip. Explicitly show the before state, a credible visual mechanism and intermediate progression, and the after state. A Type C time-lapse or montage may condense elapsed time, but it must preserve identifiable inputs, show the prerequisite stages that matter, and leave a physically and temporally plausible result. Never use Type C to excuse a missing operation, impossible assembly, unexplained object substitution, or instant transformation into a complex finished product; add distinct clips when required stages need their own visible action. Where feasible, use the preceding final frame as the before state while clearly presenting the clip as an intentional transition, not an uninterrupted take; if a different pre-transition setup is needed, state why. Preserve character identity, visual style, environment logic, important objects and their process states, color language, and lighting/camera logic where appropriate. The final state becomes the canonical start for the next clip.

A transition clip is not filler: it must communicate a meaningful change and contribute directly to the story or learning objective. When a large passage of time is needed, make it visible through a suitable mechanism such as controlled time-lapse, changing seasons or sunlight, environmental or plant growth, gradual character aging, changing weather or surroundings, motivated camera travel, or a visual match transition. For example, a young farmer planting a mango seed can lead through a visible seedling, passing seasons, the farmer's gradual aging, and tree growth to a mature mango tree; the viewer should understand the relationship between time, effort, growth, and reward.

Every Type C transition prompt must define its type, starting and ending states, what changes and what stays the same, the visual mechanism, camera behavior, exact duration sized to the transformation's complexity, requested voice-over or character dialogue and sound design, and the final state for the next clip.
